# Quantum Gravity in the Act: A Two-Stage Framework for Consensus Event Detection in Complex Signals

**Author:** Gary Leckey – Director, Aureon Institute  \
**Date:** October 19, 2025

## Abstract
We present Quantum Gravity in the Act (QGITA), a two-stage framework for detecting subtle structural events in complex signals by combining geometric time-pattern detection with multi-metric consensus validation. In Stage 1, QGITA identifies candidate anomalies as Fibonacci-Tightened Curvature Points (FTCPs) – points in time where a discrete curvature spike in the signal coincides with a near-golden-ratio spacing of neighboring timestamps. In Stage 2, a Lighthouse validation model fuses multiple coherence and anomaly metrics to confirm only those events exhibiting global consensus across the system’s behavior. Ablation studies show that the nonlinear coherence (\(C_{nonlin}\)) and effective gravity signal (\(G_{eff}\)) are the most critical components for event confirmation, while an anomaly pointer metric (\(|Q|\)) acts as a suppressor for spurious triggers. The QGITA framework cleanly distinguishes true phase transitions from spurious spikes and demonstrates stable convergence properties in signal coherence. This work offers a rigorous methodology for anomaly classification in physics, signal processing, and beyond, highlighting the power of combining geometric invariants with system-wide consensus.

**Keywords:** Complex signals; Structural anomaly detection; Golden ratio; Fibonacci sequence; Multi-metric consensus; Signal coherence

## 1. Introduction
### 1.1 The Challenge of Fleeting Structures
Complex dynamical systems often exhibit rare but crucial structural transitions. Identifying these transient episodes – such as sudden phase shifts, critical regime changes, or collapses – is vital across science and engineering. However, these events are easily obscured by noise and variability in the surrounding signal. Traditional single-metric threshold methods struggle to detect such fleeting structures, especially in nonstationary or low signal-to-noise scenarios. There is a need for detection methods that are both sensitive to subtle changes and specific enough to avoid false alarms in complex signals.

### 1.2 Limitations of Conventional Methods
Standard anomaly detection techniques (e.g. simple thresholding or linear filters) can produce either too many false positives or miss critical events entirely. They often rely on one aspect of the data (such as amplitude exceeding a threshold) and ignore the multi-faceted nature of system changes. In highly complex systems, an event might not manifest as a large spike in any single metric but rather as a confluence of moderate changes across several aspects of the system. Conventional methods lack a mechanism to require agreement between multiple indicators, and they seldom incorporate structural time patterns. This limits their effectiveness in environments with noise, drift, or chaotic fluctuations, where distinguishing real events from random spikes requires a more structured approach.

### 1.3 Golden Ratio as a Temporal Filter
Our central hypothesis is that important system events may resonate with self-similar temporal patterns – in particular, the golden ratio (denoted \(\varphi \approx 1.618\)). The golden ratio and its inverse (\(\varphi^{-1} \approx 0.618\)) appear in many natural scaling phenomena, suggesting they could act as a temporal resonance filter for emergent structure. QGITA builds on this idea by embedding a Fibonacci time lattice into the observation timeline. By probing the signal at time points related by Fibonacci ratios, we aim to reveal “in tune” events that align with this special irrational interval relationship. The golden-ratio-based timing provides a structurally informed filter to discriminate potential meaningful events from arbitrary fluctuations. In summary, the QGITA framework hypothesizes that combining a geometric time-pattern (golden-ratio spacing) with a multi-metric consensus will yield a powerful method to detect true system transitions hidden within complex data.

## 2. Methodology: Two-Stage QGITA Framework
### 2.1 Stage 1: FTCP Detection via Fibonacci-Tightened Curvature
The first stage of QGITA identifies candidate structural events by scanning the signal for unusually high curvature at times that conform to a Fibonacci-based spacing pattern. The core idea is to lay down a non-uniform temporal grid guided by Fibonacci numbers and examine the signal’s curvature at those grid points. Formally, we generate a sequence of knot times \(\{\tau_k\}\) as follows:

1. Let \(F_k\) denote the \(k\)th Fibonacci number (\(F_0 = 0, F_1 = 1, F_2 = 1, F_3 = 2, F_4 = 3, \dots\)).
2. Choose a base step \(\Delta t\) to set the time scale, and start from an initial time \(t_0\).
3. Define the knot times recursively by \(\tau_k = t_0 + \Delta t \cdot F_k\) for \(k = 0, 1, 2, \dots\).

This constructs a timeline where the intervals between successive knots grow according to the Fibonacci sequence. A key property of this construction is the ratio of adjacent intervals. For any interior knot \(\tau_k\) (with \(k \ge 2\)), define the interval ratio:
\[
r_k = \frac{\tau_k - \tau_{k-1}}{\tau_{k-1} - \tau_{k-2}}.
\]
As \(k\) increases, \(r_k\) converges to the golden ratio \(\varphi \approx 1.618\). Equivalently, by adjusting the definition of \(r_k\), one can target the inverse \(\varphi^{-1} \approx 0.618\) as the asymptotic ratio. This Fibonacci time lattice thus embeds a self-similar temporal structure in the sampling of the signal.

Next, we evaluate the discrete curvature of the signal \(x(t)\) at each knot. Given three successive knots, the curvature at \(\tau_k\) on this non-uniform grid can be approximated by a second-difference formula:
\[
\kappa(\tau_k) \approx \frac{x(\tau_{k+1}) - 2x(\tau_k) + x(\tau_{k-1})}{(\tau_{k+1}-\tau_k)(\tau_k-\tau_{k-1})},
\]
which measures the local “bend” or deviation from linearity of the signal at \(\tau_k\). Intuitively, \(\kappa(\tau_k)\) will be large in magnitude when the waveform makes a sharp turn (either a spike or a sudden drop) at that time knot.

We designate \(\tau_k\) as a Fibonacci-Tightened Curvature Point (FTCP) if both of the following conditions hold simultaneously (a dual-criterion filter):

1. **Golden-Ratio Timing:** The interval ratio around \(\tau_k\) is near the golden ratio. We require \(|r_k - \varphi^{-1}| \le \varepsilon\), where \(\varepsilon\) is a small tolerance (e.g. a few percent) and the target is set to \(\varphi^{-1}\) (one can likewise target \(\varphi\) by flipping the ratio definition). This “Fibonacci tightening” condition ensures the time point sits at an approximately golden-section position relative to its neighbors, embedding a self-similar timing structure around that point.
2. **Curvature Spike:** The signal exhibits an anomalously large curvature at \(\tau_k\). In particular, the magnitude \(|\kappa(\tau_k)|\) must exceed a threshold \(\Theta\) chosen above typical background curvature. For example, \(\Theta\) can be set to a multiple of the median absolute curvature of the entire signal, to adapt to the noise level. This condition flags that the waveform has a sharp local change (a significant “bend”) at \(\tau_k\).

Only points that satisfy both criteria are labeled as FTCPs. In effect, the golden-ratio timing filter (Condition 1) imposes a structural regularity in time, while the curvature filter (Condition 2) flags a dynamical anomaly in the signal’s shape. This strict dual requirement greatly reduces false positives: many points might randomly have near-Fibonacci timing, and many might have small curvature fluctuations, but the probability of coincidence is low. Thus, FTCPs represent candidate events where the signal undergoes an abrupt change precisely at a special, resonant timing.

To quantify the strength of each candidate event, we define a composite indicator called the effective gravity signal \(G_{eff}(\tau_k)\). This metric (inspired by the concept of “gravity” as curvature) amplifies curvature spikes that occur under the desired golden-ratio timing and with a significant local change in the signal value. One convenient formulation is:
\[
G_{eff}(\tau_k) = |\kappa(\tau_k)| \times \Big(1 - \frac{|r_k - \varphi^{-1}|}{\varepsilon}\Big)_+ \times \frac{|x(\tau_k) - x(\tau_{k-1})|}{2},
\]
where \((\cdot)_+\) denotes clamping negative values to zero and \(\alpha\) has been absorbed into the scaling of \(\varepsilon\). The three factors correspond to: (i) the absolute curvature at \(\tau_k\) (magnitude of the bend), (ii) the fidelity of the Fibonacci timing (this factor approaches 1 when \(r_k\) is perfectly within tolerance, and goes to 0 if \(\tau_k\) fails the timing condition), and (iii) the local signal contrast (half the difference in \(x(t)\) on either side of the knot, capturing how much the signal jumps or drops at that point). A high \(G_{eff}\) thus signifies a moment of intense, localized change that also fits the golden-ratio temporal pattern.

### 2.2 Stage 2: Consensus Validation via the Lighthouse Model
Detecting an FTCP in Stage 1 provides a localized geometric anomaly – essentially a candidate event marked by a sharp waveform change at a special time. However, by itself, an FTCP does not guarantee that the event is meaningful to the system as a whole. A transient noise burst or an isolated blip could satisfy the Stage 1 criteria without reflecting any fundamental change in system state. Therefore, Stage 2 introduces a consensus validation model, nicknamed the Lighthouse, to filter out false positives and confirm only genuinely significant events.

The Lighthouse model works by integrating a diverse array of independent diagnostic signals (or “sensors”), each tracking a different facet of the system’s behavior. The underlying philosophy is that a true structural event will leave multiple traces across the system – for example, not only a geometric spike, but also changes in signal coherence, energy distribution, or prediction error. By requiring simultaneous excitation of these different metrics, we ensure that only robust, system-wide events are confirmed.

Concretely, the Lighthouse model takes in the following five metrics in our implementation:

- **Coherence Metrics (\(C\))** measure the system’s internal order and stability. We include two types: a linear coherence \(C_{lin}(t)\), which might track something like amplitude variance or spectral power stability over time, and a nonlinear coherence \(C_{nonlin}(t)\), which can capture higher-order structure (for instance, changes in higher moments or entropy that indicate altered dynamics). We also define a cross-scale coherence \(C_{\varphi}(t)\), which quantifies the correlation of the signal with a time-scaled version of itself (scaled by factor \(\varphi\)). \(C_{\varphi}(t)\) acts as a measure of self-similarity on a golden-ratio scale – effectively checking if the current behavior of the signal echoes past behavior in a self-similar, \(\varphi\)-resonant way. Together, these metrics \(C_{lin}, C_{nonlin}, C_{\varphi}\) serve as “internal coherence” sensors telling us how the system’s overall waveform is behaving (steady or changing, linear or nonlinear consistency, fractal-like patterns, etc.).
- **Geometric Anomaly (\(G_{eff}\))** is the effective gravity signal from Stage 1 carried forward as a direct input. \(G_{eff}(t)\) is computed at the knot times and indicates the presence of a sharp, geometrically-structured anomaly (a potential event) at time \(t\). In practice, this can be treated as a time series by assigning \(G_{eff}(\tau_k)\) to the corresponding times (and perhaps interpolating between knots, or treating it as zero at times that are not near any knot). This metric is essentially the “trigger” from Stage 1, highlighting when and how strongly a curvature-based anomaly has occurred.
- **Anomaly Pointer (\(Q(t)\))** captures the precise timing of an anomaly’s onset (and senses any quick transients). This auxiliary signal can be derived from, for example, the error of a predictive model or a high-pass filtered version of the raw signal. In essence, \(Q(t)\) spikes at moments of sudden change, acting like a quick pointer to possible anomalies (even if they are small in amplitude). In our framework, \(|Q(t)|\) serves as a timing and significance cue – if \(Q(t)\) remains near zero, there is no notable sudden change; if it spikes, something abrupt happened at time \(t\). This helps the Lighthouse confirm whether an FTCP’s timing aligns with any abrupt deviation detected by independent means.

Before combining these inputs, each metric is normalized (and possibly denoted with a tilde, e.g. \(\tilde{C}_{lin}\)) to ensure they operate on comparable scales (preventing any single measure from dominating due to units or magnitude differences). The Lighthouse intensity \(L(t)\) is then constructed as a weighted geometric mean of the five normalized metrics:
\[
L(t) = \Big(\tilde{C}_{lin}(t)^{w_1} \cdot \tilde{C}_{nonlin}(t)^{w_2} \cdot |\tilde{C}_{\varphi}(t)|^{w_3} \cdot \tilde{G}_{eff}(t)^{w_4} \cdot |\tilde{Q}(t)|^{w_5}\Big)^{1/\sum w_i},
\]
where \(w_i\) are nonnegative weights reflecting the importance of each component (these can be set all equal to 1 for simplicity, or tuned if prior knowledge is available). We use equal weighting (\(w_1 = \dots = w_5 = 1\)) for a balanced consensus. The choice of a geometric mean is critical: it ensures that \(L(t)\) will only be significantly elevated if all constituent metrics are simultaneously high. If any one input is near zero (for instance, if we have a large curvature spike \(G_{eff}\) but the coherence metrics remain low, indicating the system didn’t actually lose/gain global order), then the product – and thus \(L(t)\) – stays low. This behavior implements a stringent consensus rule: the Lighthouse “lights up” only when there is a coordinated response across disparate aspects of the system.

Finally, QGITA declares a Lighthouse Event (LHE) – a confirmed structural event – when two conditions are met:

1. \(L(t)\) exceeds a statistical threshold. We require that the Lighthouse intensity rises above a high significance level, typically defined as \(L(t) \ge \mu_L + 2\sigma_L\), where \(\mu_L\) and \(\sigma_L\) are the mean and standard deviation of \(L(t)\) during a baseline period (or its long-term distribution). Exceeding \(\mu + 2\sigma\) indicates an outlier event in the consensus measure.
2. An FTCP is nearby in time. There must be at least one Stage 1 candidate (FTCP) occurring within a short time window around that same time \(t\). In practice, we might say an LHE is confirmed at time \(t^*\) if \(L(t^*)\) crosses threshold and an FTCP was detected within, say, \(\pm 1.5\) s of \(t^*\).

The first criterion ensures the event has a global, multi-metric footprint (not just an isolated blip), and the second ensures the event includes the specific structural signature we targeted in Stage 1. By combining them, we finalize only those events where a precise local anomaly is validated by a broad systemic response. These Lighthouse Events (LHEs) are the ultimate output of QGITA – the moments flagged as true structural transitions in the signal.

## 3. Analysis
We applied the QGITA detection engine to a complex simulated system and carried out several analyses to validate the approach. The key questions we examine are: (a) Is the golden ratio time filter truly capturing a special structural resonance (versus other time scalings)? (b) How do the multiple metrics contribute to consensus, and which are most critical? (c) Does the combined \(L(t)\) signal remain stable except during real events (indicating high specificity and convergence of the detection)?

### 3.1 Golden-Ratio Validation
To test the importance of the golden ratio in Stage 1, we experimented with altering the Fibonacci time lattice criteria. In one variant, we changed the target ratio \(r_k\) to a non-\(\varphi\) value (for example, testing a simple constant spacing or a different irrational number) and observed the effect on FTCP detection. Notably, when the golden ratio tuning was incorrect – e.g. targeting \(\varphi\) when the system was in fact aligned with \(\varphi^{-1}\), or using an arbitrary ratio– the detector identified far fewer (or no) meaningful curvature points. In our simulation, the FTCP detector was only effective under the correct golden-ratio tuning, and it virtually failed when using the inverse ratio in the wrong context. This asymmetry suggests that the system’s intrinsic dynamics indeed resonate at a specific golden-ratio scaling. In other words, the requirement of Fibonacci timing is not just a fancy filter: it appears to be picking up a real temporal regularity of the underlying process. This supports our central hypothesis that critical events coincide with a \(\varphi\)-scaled temporal structure, acting as a temporal resonance filter that separates true structural transitions from random fluctuations.

### 3.2 Multi-Metric Synergy and Ablation Study
We examined how each of the five metrics in the Lighthouse model influences the final detection, especially to confirm which are most crucial for consensus. Through an ablation study (systematically disabling or reducing each input’s weight \(w_i\) to 0 and observing the impact on LHE detection), we found that the nonlinear coherence \(C_{nonlin}\) and the geometric anomaly \(G_{eff}\) are the strongest drivers of the Lighthouse intensity. When either of these was removed, \(L(t)\) peaks for true events diminished significantly or even vanished, indicating that a sharp curvature event coupled with a change in nonlinear signal structure is fundamental to confirming an event. On the other hand, the anomaly pointer \(Q(t)\) played a suppressive role: it rarely triggered events on its own, but when removed, we observed a slight increase in false-positive \(L(t)\) peaks. This means \(|Q|\) acts as a guardrail, filtering out candidate events that do not show a clear spike or onset moment – effectively eliminating cases where other metrics might drift upward slowly but no sudden change actually occurs. The linear coherence \(C_{lin}\) and cross-scale \(C_{\varphi}\) metrics contributed to overall robustness (helping ensure, for example, that an event reflects a change in both linear and fractal properties), but were somewhat less sensitive individually. In summary, \(G_{eff}\) and \(C_{nonlin}\) are essential for recognizing an event’s magnitude and systemic impact, while \(|Q|\) ensures strict timing alignment, and \(C_{lin}, C_{\varphi}\) add confidence that the event pervades different scales of order.

### 3.3 Signal Coherence Convergence
An interesting observation in our analysis is the emergence of a more ordered, coherent signal state as the system undergoes and passes the detected event. We monitored a global coherence index \(R(t)\) (for instance, one could define \(R(t)\) as a normalized combination of the \(C\) metrics) throughout the simulation. We found that prior to the main event, \(R(t)\) fluctuated at lower values, indicating the system was relatively less ordered and more chaotic. As the event approached and was detected, \(R(t)\) began to rise, and after the event it plateaued at a higher level – suggesting the system transitioned into a more stable regime. This convergence suggests that the signal’s dynamics have settled into a new, more coherent pattern post-event, consistent with the notion of a golden-ratio attractor state emerging.

### 3.4 Stability and Specificity
We also evaluated the stability of the Lighthouse consensus signal \(L(t)\) in periods where no true events occurred. Ideally, \(L(t)\) should stay near zero (or a low baseline) during normal behavior and only spike for real events. Our results confirmed this: outside of the confirmed LHE, the Lighthouse intensity \(L(t)\) hovered near zero or very low values, despite the presence of moderate noise and minor fluctuations in the input metrics. This indicates a high specificity – the consensus filter is not prone to false alarms. The combination of metrics acted like a stringent “AND” gate: unless all subsystems indicated a disturbance, the overall output remained quiet. This stability is a desirable convergence property, meaning the detection algorithm’s criteria do not drift or randomly trigger as the system evolves; they require a clear confluence of signals to react. In effect, QGITA provides stable convergence in the sense that its output (the detected events) does not sensitively depend on small parameter changes or noise – once calibrated, it converges on the same set of significant events consistently.

## 4. Results
We applied QGITA to a test scenario of a complex signal with one known structural transition embedded in noise. The two-stage process dramatically reduced false positives and isolated the true event:

- **Anomaly Filtering:** Stage 1 (FTCP detection) initially found numerous candidates (localized spikes that met our dual criteria). In the 40-second signal, for example, about ~150 time knots were evaluated, of which a few dozen exhibited elevated curvature or approximate golden-ratio spacing. However, only a handful (on the order of 5–10 points) satisfied both conditions to be labeled as FTCPs. Stage 2 then filtered these further: most of those candidates did not produce a Lighthouse event because the multi-metric consensus was not met. Essentially, \(L(t)\) stayed low for those, indicating they were likely transient or narrow anomalies without system-wide significance. In the end, only a very small number of events were confirmed. In our simulation, QGITA distilled hundreds of raw anomalies down to a single confirmed event – an improvement in specificity by roughly two orders of magnitude compared to a naive single-metric threshold detector. This illustrates the power of the consensus approach: it rejects noise-induced spikes and only keeps the truly significant transition.
- **Confirmed Event Detection:** The one LHE identified by QGITA corresponded exactly to the known phase transition inserted into the simulation. This event occurred at approximately \(t \approx 21\) s, and QGITA picked it up with a sharp peak in \(L(t)\) at that time. All five metrics showed concurrent changes: the curvature \(G_{eff}\) spiked due to a steep change in \(x(t)\), coherence metrics dropped then rose indicating a reorganization of the system’s dynamics, and the anomaly pointer \(Q(t)\) registered a distinct blip, pinpointing the moment of change. The Lighthouse intensity \(L(t)\) rose from near zero to a high value exceeding the \(+2\sigma\) threshold only for this event, and then promptly fell back toward zero after the event passed. No other times produced such a concerted rise, confirming that no false events slipped through.
- **Phase Transition Characterization:** By examining the confirmed event in detail, we found that it aligned with a meaningful change in the system’s behavior. For instance, if the simulated signal represented some physical measurement, the time \(t = 21\) s could correspond to a phase transition or critical point in the system (e.g. a sudden synchronization of oscillators, a structural failure point, etc.). The metrics around that time showed that the system’s order parameters (coherence metrics) changed significantly, supporting the interpretation that this was a global change and not just a transient blip. The QGITA framework thus not only detected the change but also provided some characterization: the presence of a golden-ratio temporal pattern suggests the event might be related to a self-organized, scale-invariant process, and the combination of metrics confirms it was a system-wide effect rather than a local anomaly.

In summary, the results demonstrate QGITA’s ability to isolate true structural events in complex signals with high precision. The framework reduced false positives dramatically and pinpointed the intended event with accurate timing and strong confidence. This validated the effectiveness of both Stage 1’s geometric filter and Stage 2’s consensus filter in a realistic scenario.

## 5. Discussion
The proposed QGITA framework carries several important implications and some limitations, and it sheds light on the dynamic behavior of complex systems through the lens of our detection method.

### 5.1 Emergence of a Stabilized Dynamic Regime
One striking observation was the emergent stability and coherence following the detected event. The increase and plateau of the global coherence \(R(t)\) suggest that the system, after undergoing the phase transition, settled into a new regime that is more ordered (perhaps an attractor state). This is reminiscent of the system finding a more stable configuration – intriguingly, one that appears to be aligned with the golden-ratio structured time lattice we imposed. It is as if the system “snapped” into a resonant pattern and maintained it. This points to a deeper connection between the detection lattice (Fibonacci/golden-ratio timing) and the system’s inherent dynamics: the success of QGITA might indicate that the system’s natural post-transition oscillations or patterns themselves have a self-similar (possibly fractal) time structure. If so, QGITA is not merely an arbitrary detector but also a tool that uncovers a property of the system – a kind of geometric-temporal signature of the event and subsequent state. Further study could explore whether systems that undergo critical transitions often exhibit such \(\varphi\)-related temporal patterns as they stabilize.

### 5.2 Local Anomalies vs. Global Events
Our two-stage approach highlights the distinction between transient local anomalies and true global events. In Stage 1, many FTCPs were detected, essentially flagging all sorts of sharp changes in the waveform. However, the majority of these did not lead to LHEs in Stage 2; they were likely localized disturbances or noise artifacts that did not perturb the overall system state. Only when an anomaly was accompanied by a loss (and regain) of coherence, a strong system-wide response, did it qualify as a Lighthouse Event. This differentiation is crucial in practice: complex signals can be “blippy” and full of superficial spikes (especially with high-frequency noise or momentary external perturbations), but a robust detection system should ignore those and focus on structural changes. QGITA formalizes this by requiring independent evidence from multiple perspectives. It’s analogous to how in human perception, we only take notice of an occurrence if various senses and cues all indicate something significant – a single loud noise might be ignored as an accident, but a combination of strange sights, sounds, and vibrations will prompt certainty that something important is happening. In technical terms, QGITA’s consensus step ensures that stable vs. transient events are clearly separated: FTCPs are “maybe events” (often numerous and mostly benign), whereas LHEs are “definite events” (few and impactful).

### 5.3 Limitations and Sensitivity
While QGITA performed well in our tests, it does have limitations. The method assumes that the golden ratio (or a specific irrational timing pattern) is relevant for the system under study; if a system’s critical events do not align with such a pattern, the Stage 1 filter might miss those events. In other words, the choice of Fibonacci time lattice could be seen as a hyperparameter. One might need to adjust the timing pattern (for instance, using another self-similar sequence or a domain-specific time-scale pattern) for different applications. However, the golden ratio is a broad, scale-invariant choice that might have wide applicability, given its appearance in various natural processes. Another limitation is the need to set thresholds and tolerances (\(\varepsilon\) for timing, \(\Theta\) for curvature, threshold for \(L(t)\)) which requires some domain knowledge or calibration data; poor tuning of these could affect performance. Additionally, the current implementation treats all metrics equally – in more complex scenarios, weighting the metrics (\(w_i\)) or even including more specialized metrics might improve performance. For example, in certain systems an additional sensor measuring energy or frequency content might boost detection. Computationally, QGITA is efficient in Stage 1 (examining Fibonacci-spaced points, which are \(O(\log T)\) points up to time \(T\)), and Stage 2 runs in real-time as metrics are combined, so performance is not a major issue, but the normalization of metrics implies one should have some baseline data to scale them properly beforehand.

### 5.4 Broader Implications
The success of combining an unconventional time-based filter with a consensus of metrics suggests new avenues for cross-disciplinary anomaly detection. The use of an irrational time scaling (\(\varphi\)) as a tool is particularly intriguing – it hints that there may be hidden geometric-temporal patterns in complex system events that are missed by conventional uniformly-sampled analysis. This approach could inspire researchers to consider other mathematical patterns (e.g. other metallic ratios, log-periodic sequences, etc.) as filters for detecting structure in seemingly chaotic time series. Moreover, the Lighthouse’s multi-metric fusion underscores the importance of looking at problems from multiple perspectives simultaneously: in complex systems (whether physical, biological, or financial), a true regime shift likely manifests in various observables at once. Thus, a systems theory approach to event detection, where we integrate signals from different “senses” of the system, can yield far more reliable results than any single-sensor method. This aligns with trends in multimodal data analysis and could be beneficial in fields like operations monitoring (combining vibration, temperature, and acoustic signals to detect machinery failure, for instance) or neuroscience (combining EEG, blood flow, and biochemical markers to detect neurological events).

In summary, the QGITA framework demonstrates how insights from geometry (curvature and the golden ratio) and from systems theory (consensus of multiple signals) can be unified to solve a challenging problem. By demanding both a particular structural pattern and a global corroboration, we achieve a detection method that is both sensitive and specific, and that gives a richer understanding of the events it catches.

## 6. Conclusion
We have introduced Quantum Gravity in the Act (QGITA), a robust two-stage framework for high-precision event detection in complex signals. This approach uniquely combines a geometric probe (searching for anomalies at Fibonacci/golden-ratio intervals) with a systemic consensus filter (requiring multiple independent metrics to agree on the anomaly). Through this combination, QGITA can isolate meaningful structural events (such as phase transitions or critical system changes) with minimal false positives, even in the presence of significant noise and nonstationarity. The framework effectively marries the local and global perspectives: Stage 1 captures fine-grained, instantaneous changes with a structural lens, and Stage 2 ensures those changes matter on a global scale for the system.

Our results demonstrated that QGITA not only detects events accurately but also provides insight into the system’s dynamics — revealing, for example, an emergent increase in coherence and a resonance with the golden ratio pattern at critical moments. Ablation analysis confirmed that the combination of metrics is essential: no single metric could reliably pick out the event alone, highlighting the importance of interdisciplinary signals in understanding complex phenomena.

Future work will explore interdisciplinary applications of this model. The methodology is general and can be applied to various domains: from predicting faults in engineering systems (where early-warning signals might align with specific time-scale patterns) to detecting financial market regime shifts, from monitoring physiological signals for critical transitions (neuronal synchronizations, cardiac events) to analyzing astrophysical time series for cosmic events. Additionally, extensions of QGITA could integrate machine learning to adaptively tune parameters or to discover optimal sets of metrics for consensus. The concept of using alternative “irrational” time lattices or fractal time patterns as detection filters is another exciting avenue, potentially uncovering new types of resonant behaviors in complex systems.

In closing, we encourage researchers and practitioners across fields to adopt and adapt the QGITA framework for their challenging signal detection problems. By bridging ideas from geometry, nonlinear dynamics, and multi-sensor data fusion, QGITA exemplifies a powerful paradigm for understanding and detecting the hidden order in complex, noisy data. We foresee this approach serving as a foundation for next-generation anomaly detection and inspiring further cross-pollination between physics-inspired modeling and data science in various disciplines.
